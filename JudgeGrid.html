§0 The Thesis

Menlo Ventures' December 2025 report estimated $12.5 billion of enterprise foundation-model API spend in 2025, while reporting rapid price declines at comparable quality. This remains useful as a dated 2025 market baseline for why cost-aware routing matters, not as a live 2026 total or a measurement from this project. The defensible question for an enterprise is therefore: "which model is best and cheapest for this specific task - and is that still true after this week's release?" No public leaderboard identified in this review answers it at enterprise-task granularity.

This document proposes the operation to build one.

The problem we solve

Enterprise AI teams often face a version of the same planning problem: they cannot confidently say which model should run which task, or what a workload will cost and deliver. This plan treats the resulting tendency to over-provision frontier models as a hypothesis to test, not a universal behavioral claim.

Published reports frame the surrounding market and adoption risks. The list combines current 2025-2026 reports with historical benchmark comparisons; it is not a head-to-head dataset or a pooled estimate.

  • Gartner's January 26, 2026 analysis reports that at least 50% of generative-AI projects were abandoned after proof of concept by the end of 2025, citing poor data quality, inadequate risk controls, escalating costs, or unclear business value. This is Gartner's reported estimate rather than a census; it is a current adoption-risk signal, not a universal failure rate.
  • McKinsey's November 5, 2025 State of AI research reports widespread generative-AI adoption but limited reported enterprise-level EBIT impact for many organizations. This is a dated survey finding, not a universal causal result or a current ROI forecast.
  • Menlo Ventures reported model-API spend of $8.4B by mid-2025 and $12.5B for 2025 in separate dated reports. Stanford's 2025 AI Index reported a more than 280× decline over approximately 18 months for a GPT-3.5-level MMLU system, from November 2022 to October 2024. These figures are historical and benchmark-specific; they support the routing question but do not establish a current 2026 market total.

The unanswerable question - "which model, at what cost, delivering what quality, for this task?" - is an adoption blocker hiding in plain sight.

Published evidence includes grounding and reliability failures (separable failure modes to measure)

Published enterprise-agent benchmarks have shown grounding and reliability failures, but their numbers are version-, model-, and protocol-specific. On CMU's TheAgentCompany, the paper reports roughly 30% end-to-end task success for its best tested agent under its stated protocol; Sierra's τ-bench repository reports airline pass^4 values that vary by strategy and model. These are historical external results, not measurements of this plan. The operational hypothesis is that grounding is a separable failure mode worth measuring.

The three vignettes below are illustrative, composite failure patterns in the spirit of what those benchmarks record.

🖥️ A customer asks about a monitor's max resolution.

The model writes an excellent general troubleshooting guide - but misses the product-specific facts in the actual user manual: it implies USB-C can carry 4K (it can't, for this monitor) and never recommends the DisplayPort/HDMI cable the manual specifies. It knew about monitors; it didn't know this monitor.

Lesson: Grounding failure, not reasoning - the model knew the category but not the specific product manual.

🗂️ An agent is asked to find the "most popular" motherboard.

The orders-search tool returns 20 results per call. The model hits the cap on all four motherboards and punts the question to a human, never realizing it could paginate. A cheaper, purpose-built query workflow would have finished the job.

Lesson: Grounding/retrieval failure - the model didn't realize it could paginate the search.

📦 A support ticket about a cooling system.

The model searches the knowledge base with long, specific phrases ("Sample Monitor Pro", "cooling fan not starting"), gets nothing back, and - instead of retrieving the shipment record - hallucinates a delivery date by reading the shipment ID as a date (ship-2026-0214-… → "delivered February 14"). The reference record says February 22. A return the customer was owed got denied.

Lesson: Grounding failure - hallucinated a date from an ID instead of retrieving the record.

The working hypothesis here is not simply "we need a better frontier model." It is that some tasks may be better served by a specialist. Grounding - reading the right document, querying the right record, paginating the right search - is a separable skill, and historical routing studies show that cheaper models can match or approach stronger models in specific benchmark settings. That evidence does not establish a production result for this plan.

The wedge: frontier overkill, and the composition thesis

Here is where it gets economically interesting. Published evaluations often show frontier systems leading on difficult coding and reasoning tasks, but that does not imply they are the best fit for every workload. For retrieval, grounding, OCR, extraction, classification, and some summarization, this plan tests whether specialized and smaller models can match acceptable quality at lower cost:

  • BEST-Route (ICML 2025) reports cost reductions of up to 60% with less than a 1% performance drop on its real-world datasets by choosing both the model and the number of responses based on query difficulty and quality thresholds. This is a study-specific result, not a production guarantee; re-run the comparison on the target workload.
  • Routing is a cost-optimization hypothesis that this project proposes to test under enterprise task rubrics. Any production savings claim must be recomputed from measured tokens, a dated price manifest, workload mix, and the applicable provider terms.

And the deeper insight - the one this benchmark is built to surface - is composition. You don't have to pick one model. A retrieval/grounding specialist can align and ground a frontier model: the specialist pulls the right facts; the frontier model reasons over them. Whether that improves accuracy and cost is an empirical question for the planned rubric. Model catalogs on AWS Bedrock and Google Cloud provide multiple provider/model options; the missing piece is knowing which combination wins for which task at what cost. A current coding-specific example, the 2025 AdaptiveLLM preprint, reports a 7.86% pass@1 improvement with 88.9% lower resource consumption than its ComplexityNet baseline, plus an approximately 15% accuracy improvement at similar cost versus a single model. That is a task-specific preprint result for difficulty-aware selection, not a production claim or evidence that composition always wins.

Composition thesis A retrieval/grounding specialist can align and ground a frontier model: the specialist pulls the right facts; the frontier model reasons over them. The hypothesis is that composition may improve quality and/or cost on the target workload; the benchmark must measure whether it does.

No public leaderboard identified in this review provides that exact combination; this is a bounded review finding, not an exhaustive market census.

What this plan proposes

This static document proposes an enterprise human-calibrated model evaluation and cost/performance routing benchmark and leaderboard - a future continuously-updated, rubric-grounded, cost-aware read on which model (and which model combination) wins each enterprise task type at the lowest cost. The current τ-bench repository describes later τ²/τ³-bench work with additional domains and modalities; this plan treats that as repository status to re-check at each release, not as a permanent market fact. Each planned release would answer the operational question every AI lead actually asks: "A new model just dropped - for each of our task types, is it better or cheaper than what we run today, and should we switch?" This file is not a live benchmark, measurement product, leaderboard, or source of current benchmark results.

A note on scope (why six task types, not coding only)

A coding-agent leaderboard is the narrow version of this problem: benchmark new coding-agent releases, rank them, stop. This plan instead proposes enterprise cost/performance routing across specialized task types, with coding as the flagship category - a form that may fit organizations with heterogeneous workloads, and a response to the public failure pattern (frontier models failing at grounding, §0). Coding is deeply represented throughout (authoring, grading, statistics, the illustrative leaderboard), and the operational machinery could support a coding-only variant.

§1 Benchmark Design

This section specifies what we measure and how we score it. The operation that produces it (a mixed pool of about 1,000 qualified experts, roles, and pipeline) is Section 2; the per-task instructions contributors get are Section 3.

The hybrid scoring model

The whole point of the benchmark is to produce a quality signal that is auditable enough to inform routing decisions. The proposed hybrid scoring model pairs an LLM judge grading every response against every rubric criterion at scale with human raters grading a stratified gold sample each release to calibrate the judge, adjudicate disagreements, and catch judge failures. The judge scales the volume; humans provide calibration evidence. No scoring process guarantees trust without measured agreement, audit results, and release-specific review. Concretely, in order:

Layer 1 - LLM judge scores every candidate response against every rubric criterion. Each criterion is a verifiable assertion (✅/❌) and the reward = proportion of criteria satisfied. Criteria decompose along four axes: Completeness, Correctness, Constraint-Satisfaction, Format-Compliance. A deterministic checker may override only the criterion it covers; the system records the judge score, checker result, and final precedence decision for every criterion. Because criteria are granular and objective (grounded in environment state rather than a vibe-judgment), judge reliability is a target measured by Layer 3/4 calibration, not a guaranteed property. This is the volume scorer: near-zero marginal human labor per additional trajectory, excluding API and infrastructure spend.

Layer 2 - Auto-score floor (deterministic checks override the judge). Where a criterion is deterministically checkable, the auto-scorer's verdict overrides the judge's - it's more reliable and free:

  • Coding: a task is resolved iff the candidate patch passes every test in FAIL_TO_PASS (failed before, passes after) and every test in PASS_TO_PASS (passed before, still passes after) - the SWE-bench definition. For long-horizon tasks, this deterministic result is the floor; a feature-completeness rubric (§2.8) layers on top, and the recorded precedence contract identifies which checker owns each criterion.
  • OCR: Character Error Rate, CER = (substitutions + deletions + insertions) / reference_chars, with an explicit policy for empty references; field-level exact match for structured fields.
  • Extraction: strict entity-level F1 - true positive only if both span and type match, following the exact-span evaluation convention used in CoNLL-2003.
  • Classification: macro-F1 (unweighted per-class mean) and MCC (robust to imbalance; report both - MCC alone can mislead under extreme imbalance).

Layer 3 - Human gold sample (the human-calibration layer). Each release draws a ~10-15% stratified gold sample for independent human rating. One task-endorsed expert rates every sampled trajectory; a ~20% calibration core receives two additional independent ratings. The assignment envelope also reserves capacity for blind re-rates and auto-scorer audits (§2.2, §7.3). Humans check grounding against the PII-stripped source document. Adjudicated human evidence is the system of record on sampled, non-deterministic criteria; the judge owns unsampled criteria, calibrated by that evidence.

Layer 4 - Judge recalibration (the loop that keeps the judge honest). Human ratings on the gold sample become the calibration signal: judge-human disagreement is diagnosed per criterion before a rubric or prompt is changed (§2.3). The proposed ~10% hand audit of auto-scored items and blind re-rates are nested within the 1.6 human-assignment envelope; adjudication is budgeted separately. OpenAI's February 23, 2026 SWE-bench Verified audit is a primary example of why test and grader validity require continuing review, not evidence of a universal error rate.

Terminology and precedence. In this plan, a gold sample is a human-calibration subset of trajectories; a gold item is a pre-answered QA sentinel used to monitor a rater; a reference answer is an authoring artifact used to define rubric criteria; and a calibration core is the subset receiving three independent human ratings. The judge scores every criterion, then the per-criterion owner is ordered explicitly: (1) deterministic checker when the criterion is covered, (2) adjudicated human gold result when sampled, (3) LLM-judge result otherwise. final_grade/scoring_path record that decision and any checker scope.

The net effect: the judge makes the operation scale (100% of volume at near-zero marginal human labor, while API and infrastructure costs remain), and humans make it trustworthy (deep ~1hr reviews on the sample that calibrates the judge and catches its failures).

Cost metrics

Cost is a first-class axis, reported alongside quality for every model on every task type. Two complementary units, following the Artificial Analysis methodology:

  • Primary - $/task (USD): computed from measured token consumption (input + output + reasoning + cache-hit + cache-write) at snapshot prices, averaged over the k runs. This is the routing-decision unit - what an enterprise actually pays per unit of work. Rates differ by provider, model, token category, execution mode, region, and timestamp; consumption-per-task also varies by model verbosity and tokenizer generation. So $/task is computed from measured tokens and the release manifest, never quoted prices alone.
  • Secondary - Blended $/1M tokens (a fixed cache:input:output blend): for cross-model scanning and comparability with other leaderboards.
  • Reported per execution mode: standard, cached, and batch. Prompt caching and batch APIs can materially change enterprise economics; discount percentages, eligibility, cache windows, regions, model IDs, and provider terms must be rechecked in the price manifest before each release rather than copied from a static example.
  • Always with latency: per-run TTFT/tokens-sec observations plus aggregate wall-clock p50/p95 computed from those observations. Latency is a routing constraint (enterprise apps have budgets), not just a cost.
  • Reasoning-token column (dedicated): where a provider exposes reasoning or thinking tokens, record them separately and apply that model's documented billing rule. Some providers count them as output tokens; telemetry and billing treatment are provider- and model-specific. In the synthetic §6 sample row, thinking tokens outnumber visible output ~6:1, illustrating why measured usage matters.

Measurement & versioning. Token counts come from provider telemetry (for example, AWS Bedrock's TokenUsage schema requires input/output totals but makes cache fields optional). Every priced trajectory carries the content hash of its original price-manifest JSON (model_id, provider, input/output/cache/batch prices, execution context, timestamp). Published cost and the original manifest remain immutable. A separately labeled normalized_cost_usd may be recomputed from measured usage under a newer shared manifest; missing telemetry is reported through coverage fields, not silently treated as zero. Quality scores are never edited retroactively.

A nuance the research surfaced: temperature=0 is not deterministic (arXiv:2408.04667 found accuracy varying up to 15% across repeated runs of "deterministic" configurations, with request batching a suspected cause), so token counts vary run-to-run. Cost is always reported as a mean over k runs with a CI, never a single observation.

Routing & composition methodology

This is the benchmark's payoff: a defensible answer to "should we switch?" per task type.

Primary routing row - quality plus reliability gates. For each task type, the enterprise pre-registers a quality threshold T and a task-specific reliability floor. Routing enums are deterministic: recommended is the cheapest arm whose 95% CI lower bound clears T and whose reliability floor passes; alternative is another arm that clears both gates (for example, the highest-quality qualified arm); not_recommended is any arm below either gate. A below-gate composition is therefore not an alternative merely because it is interesting.

Three composition patterns

The topology diagram compares three illustrative composition patterns: a retrieval specialist grounds a frontier model; a small model drafts and a stronger model verifies; and a cheap model escalates uncertain cases to an expensive model. The cascade pattern is informed by the FrugalGPT study.

Composition arms - the key insight beyond single-model routing. Where a model combination plausibly could outperform a single model, we benchmark it as a first-class arm. Each composition's total $/task is the summed cost across participating calls; its quality is graded on the same rubric. Cost uses measured arm-level calls, not copied solo-row costs. Latency is end-to-end wall clock for each complete composition run; p50/p95 are computed from those complete-run observations and are never obtained by adding component percentiles. A cascade uses the base call plus the observed cost of escalated cases. Examples:

  • Specialist-grounds-frontier: a cheap retrieval/grounding model pulls the right facts; the frontier model reasons over them. (This is the §0 monitor/reference fix - the frontier model stops hallucinating shipment dates because a specialist handed it the right record.)
  • Drafter + verifier: a small model drafts code/answers; a stronger model verifies. It can be cheaper than running the strong model alone when the verifier call is bounded; the benchmark measures whether quality holds.
  • Cascade: cheap model first, escalate to expensive on low confidence (FrugalGPT-style).

Each release reports whether a composition (if any) would beat the best single model on cost-adjusted quality for each task type - the frontier-overkill + grounding thesis made measurable rather than an assumed outcome.

Repeated-run reliability guardrail - pass^k. We report pass^1 (headline solved%), mean quality (for partial-credit ranking), and task-specific pass^k: following the precise τ-bench definition, the probability that all k independent trials of the same task succeed. The approximation pass^k ≈ (pass^1)^k requires independent, identically distributed trials, so the release reports the empirical all-k rate whenever possible. This metric measures consistency across repeated end-to-end trials; it does not by itself estimate failure compounding across sequential tool calls inside one trajectory. A routing row is not_recommended when its configured reliability floor is not met, even if its pass^1 leads.

Statistical design (with the honest trade-off)

This is where most benchmark plans hand-wave. We won't.

The detectable-gap problem, stated honestly. Under the standard normal approximation for two independent proportions, a two-sided 5% test, 80% power, and a worst-case baseline near 0.50, detecting a 2 percentage-point gap requires roughly 9,800 instances per arm. The planned ~600-950 instances per task type supports only materially larger gaps (roughly ~5-8 points, depending on baseline and design). Recompute power for paired outcomes, clustering, multiplicity, attrition, and the actual baseline before each release.

So we make the trade-off explicit and defensible:

Sample sizes per task type: Coding 750, RAG 650, Summarization 600 (the types with the largest human/judge rubric load per item - coding's tests are auto-scored, but its feature-completeness rubric and gold audit are not), Extraction/OCR 950 each, Classification 900 (near-fully auto-graded - push the n up). ~4,800 instances total per release (600-950/type; ≈800/type average).

What this buys: reliable detection of meaningful gaps (~5pp+ on the binary metrics at mid-range rates, smaller on continuous ones, where a few hundred instances suffice for a 1-5 mean). That's the right resolution for routing decisions - enterprises don't switch models for a 2pp difference; they switch for the 10-30pp gaps this benchmark is built to surface. If a release produces a near-tie, we say so rather than fake-rank it.

Uncertainty reporting: use stratified bootstrap 95% CIs for quality and for $/task when observed costs are skewed, documenting the resampling unit and dependence structure. Increase resamples for adjacent-rank decisions and report paired differences rather than relying on overlapping marginal intervals. Use a prespecified paired test appropriate to the endpoint (for example, McNemar for paired binary outcomes); absent that rule, report estimates and intervals without an ordinal claim. (A 2022 LREC methodological paper is retained as historical precedent, not current model evidence; it argues for publishing bootstrap CIs and differences rather than bare SOTA claims.)

Agent runs - differentiated by stochasticity, not uniform. Leading agentic benchmarks have moved past "one run per task": among passing SWE-bench trajectories, ~10.7% are "lucky passes" (AgentLens, arXiv:2605.12925), which is exactly why τ-bench introduced the pass^k metric (arXiv:2406.12045) and why AI21 ran SWE-bench 200,000+ times at duplicity 4-16 to measure variance (ai21.com); BrowseComp used 64 trials/task for Deep Research and o1 (OpenAI). So we set k by task type, not globally:

  • Stochastic / agentic (Coding, multi-turn RAG, agent-style composition arms): k=5 runs, report pass^k (all-k-succeed reliability) + mean - the lucky-pass-prone cases.
  • Semi-stochastic (Summarization, single-turn RAG): k=3 runs, report mean + pass^1.
  • Deterministic (Classification MCQ, exact-match Extraction/OCR): k=1 suffices - output is near-constant, so re-running buys nothing but cost. (This is why those task types get the highest per-type n - 900-950 - instead: spend the budget on items, not re-runs.)

Why not just buy more n? Because the operation is human-grounded on ~10-15% of trajectory volume (plus continuous authoring), and §7's budget math shows the cost curve. The benchmark is designed to the budget an enterprise would actually pay, not an infinite-n academic ideal. We name the resolution limit; we don't hide it.

Anti-contamination & versioning

A public benchmark with public answers gets trained on - contamination can inflate scores (one published study found materially different effects across coding benchmarks; arXiv:2407.07565), and OpenAI stopped reporting SWE-bench Verified in its February 23, 2026 publication after identifying contamination and flawed tests. OpenAI's July 8, 2026 follow-up also withdrew its earlier recommendation to adopt SWE-bench Pro. These are reasons for controls, not proof that this proposed design is contamination-proof:

  1. Private gold. Task prompts and reference answers are never published. The leaderboard releases aggregate scores plus a handful of redacted worked examples. (GAIA withholds ~300 of 466 answers server-side for the same reason.)
  2. Two pools per release - 60/40 anchor/rotate. ~60% of tasks form a fixed anchor set for cross-release comparability (is the new model actually better, or did the test get easier?); ~40% are fresh tasks authored each cycle to resist memorization - the SWE-bench-Live / LiveCodeBench rolling-cutoff pattern.
  3. Temporal cutoff tagging. Every task carries an authoring date; for a given model's row we suppress tasks predating its known training cutoff where that cutoff is documented. This reduces contamination risk; it cannot prove that a model never saw the text.
  4. Canary strings in the style documented by the BIG-bench repository, embedded in held-out task content with periodic overlap audits. Canaries are leak-detection signals, not proof that a task was unseen.
  5. Standardized harness. Every competing model runs through the same harness, prompts, and reasoning configuration within a fixed comparison view. The official SWE-bench site illustrates the distinction: its Bash Only view standardizes the mini-SWE-agent environment, while the broader leaderboard includes varied agent systems.
  6. Versioned leaderboard. Every row carries (benchmark_version, task_set_hash, scoring_protocol_hash, n, CI, price_manifest_hash, execution_mode, k). Quality comparison requires matching benchmark version, task-set hash, scoring protocol, and k. Direct published-cost comparison additionally requires matching price manifest and execution mode; rows from different manifests may be cost-compared only after separate normalization to one shared manifest. Historical rows remain immutable.

§2 The Operation: Roles & Pipeline

Section 1 said what we measure. This section says who does it and how it flows. The operation has four workstreams - one automated, three human:

  • An LLM judge grades every response at scale against expert-authored atomic rubric criteria (the volume scorer; near-zero marginal human labor, excluding API and infrastructure spend).
  • A human workforce authors tasks + rubrics + world/source data - the long pole, run continuously between releases.
  • Human raters grade a stratified gold sample each release to calibrate the judge, adjudicate disagreements, and catch judge failures (the human-calibration layer).
  • Adjudication + judge-auditing on ties and judge-vs-human disagreements feeds a recalibration loop.

The judge scales the grading; humans make the grading trustworthy. The design obeys one non-negotiable rule: every workflow layer must justify its own existence - no redundant review.

Two tiers: a fixed enablement core + a variable mixed-expert bench

This is not "1,000 evaluators billing every release." It's a small full-time core that runs the pipeline, drawing on a mixed pool for variable work. Software engineers qualify for coding; domain-qualified evaluators cover RAG, summarization, OCR, extraction, and classification. Every contributor passes a general rubric qualification and earns task-type endorsements before receiving that queue.

This split is what makes the budget defensible: fixed core (~6 FTE) + variable bench billed by the hour, instead of 1,000 standing graders.

The branch is load-bearing: the judge grades 100% of volume; humans grade only the ~10-15% gold sample, then feed calibration back to the judge.

  1. LLM judge scores every candidate response against every rubric criterion. Each rubric criterion is a verifiable assertion ("the response should state the 14-day return window"); the judge emits a per-criterion ✅/❌; reward = proportion of criteria satisfied. Criteria are intended to be granular and grounded in checkable state, while reliability is measured by the calibration loop below.
  2. Auto-score floor (coding/extraction/OCR/classification). Where a criterion is deterministically checkable (test pass/fail, CER, F1, MCC), the auto-scorer's verdict is first precedence and overrides the judge for that criterion only; for sampled non-deterministic criteria, the adjudicated human gold result is next, otherwise the judge owns the result. The recorded scoring_path and precedence decision identify the owner. The judge still scores every criterion so disagreement can be measured.
  3. Stratified gold sample → budgeted human controls. Each release draws a gold sample (~10-15% of trajectories, stratified across task types, models, and judge-confidence bands). One task-endorsed expert rates every sampled trajectory; a ~20% calibration core receives two additional independent ratings. That is 1.4 assignments per sampled trajectory. The remaining 0.2 trajectory-equivalent assignments in the 1.6 envelope fund blind re-rates and the 10% auto-scorer audit; ~5% gold probes are embedded in normal queues. Adjudication remains a separate, explicitly budgeted line.
  4. Adjudication on disagreement. Where calibration-core raters split, or where a sampled human rating disagrees with the judge, a senior Adjudicator breaks the tie. One tier.
  5. Judge recalibration (the human-feedback calibration loop). Human ratings on the gold sample become the calibration signal: systematic disagreement is diagnosed across the rubric, judge prompt, task mix, and human panel; rewrite or re-prompt only when the evidence supports that intervention.

Why this isn't redundant: the judge grades 100% of volume at near-zero marginal human labor (API and infrastructure spend still apply); humans grade a ~10-15% gold sample where their judgment uniquely matters (calibration, adjudication, failure-catching). Sampling overlaps are intentional controls, and their assignments are included in the modeled human volume in §7 rather than treated as free work.

Inter-annotator agreement (IAA) - now including judge-vs-human

IAA is the lever that keeps the operation honest. We track it at the item level, for both human raters and the judge.

  • Metric: Krippendorff's α. The commonly cited α ≥ 0.80 and 0.667 ≤ α < 0.80 bands are operating guidelines, not universal pass/fail laws; interpretation depends on sample size, prevalence, consequences, and measurement level.
  • The diagnostic rule: low item-level α triggers review of rubric ambiguity, rater error, class prevalence, and task difficulty. Rewrite only when the evidence points to the item; coach or requalify raters when the disagreement is rater-specific, and collect more observations when the interval is too wide.
  • Judge-human agreement is tracked per criterion per release; a low or uncertain estimate triggers diagnosis before judge re-prompting, rubric revision, or additional sampling.
  • Per-rater longitudinal tracking: drift triggers re-qualification (§4.5) before it corrupts a release.
  • Aggregation: the published leaderboard uses the judge's per-criterion scores (calibrated, transparent). Internally, MACE / Dawid-Skene on the human gold sample estimates rater competence and flags compromised raters.

The data-provision pipeline (the overlooked component)

RAG and OCR grounding cannot be evaluated reliably without the source document the model was supposed to use, PII-stripped and aligned to the response. This is a real workstream the enablement core owns. The pipeline:

  1. Ingest real source documents - KB articles, support transcripts, product manuals, forms, order records - from the enterprise data (or from a simulated world DB for benchmark scenarios).
  2. PII detection + redaction - named-entity + pattern-based (emails, phones, account IDs, payment info); replace with stable synthetic tokens so referential integrity is preserved (the reviewer still sees "Customer A," just not a person's name or street address). Redaction is itself QA'd: a sample of redacted docs is reviewed for both under-redaction (PII leaked) and over-redaction (signal destroyed). Ingest and processing require documented authorization, access controls, retention/deletion rules, and jurisdiction-specific privacy review.
  3. Alignment to the response - package each rating task as (prompt, PII-stripped source doc(s), model response, rubric), so the rater can check grounding claim-by-claim against the actual source (the §0 monitor scenario: the rater needs the sample manual to see that "USB-C supports 4K" is wrong).
  4. Provenance + versioning - every rating task carries a source_doc_hash and redaction version, so a release is reproducible and a redaction bug is traceable.

This is also where the coding long-horizon rubric layer lives (§2.8).

Coding: tests as the floor, rubric as the ceiling

For coding, auto-tests (resolved%, §1.2) are the pass/fail floor - does it run and pass the known-good tests. But for long-horizon tasks (multi-file features, refactors, integrations), tests catch "does it run," not "did it actually implement all the features correctly and function end-to-end." So coding uses a two-layer score:

  • Layer 1 (auto, floor): SWE-bench-style resolved% (FAIL_TO_PASS + PASS_TO_PASS). Fails here = task failed, full stop.
  • Layer 2 (rubric, ceiling): expert-authored criteria for feature completeness and correct function on top of passing tests - "the refactor removes the deprecated API from all 4 call sites," "the new endpoint validates input X," "the migration handles the empty-set edge case." These are graded by the judge (calibrated against the human gold sample), exactly like the other task types, decomposed along the same Completeness/Correctness/Constraint-Satisfaction/Format axes.

It's why coding authoring cost is high - you build the repo, the tests, and the feature-completeness rubric.

§3 Worker-Facing Task Instructions

This section is the document a contributor receives - not a description of one. It is written in second person. In production it would live in the task platform; here it is reproduced in full.

Task instructions - Model Evaluation

Your role, and why it matters

You are part of a team evaluating how well AI models perform real enterprise work - answering customer questions from company documents, summarizing reports, extracting data from forms, writing and fixing code. Your rating decides which models get recommended to enterprises for which tasks. A wrong rating doesn't just cost us time - it can steer a real company toward a model that hallucinates a refund policy or ships broken code. That is why accuracy matters more than speed here, and why your pay includes a quality bonus tied to how often you agree with the gold answers and with your fellow raters (§2.4).

You will work in one of three tracks on any given task:

  • Authoring - writing realistic tasks + atomic rubrics + reference answers + gold (the quality ceiling for the whole benchmark).
  • Gold-sample rating - the deep (~1 hour) review of a (prompt, PII-stripped source, model response) triple against the rubric, on the stratified sample that calibrates our automated judge. This is where most bench rating hours go.
  • Judge-auditing - senior raters review cases where the automated judge and the human panel disagree, identify the failure pattern (was the rubric ambiguous? the judge wrong? the response genuinely borderline?), and feed the fix back (rewrite the item, or re-prompt the judge).

The judge grades 100% of model responses automatically; your human rating provides independent calibration evidence. That is why reviews are deep and sampled: the pilot must establish that they are accurate enough to support release decisions.

The gold-sample rating job - how to review a model response

You will see four things on every rating task:

  1. The task prompt - what the model was asked to do (e.g. "Answer the customer's question using only the knowledge-base article provided").
  2. The PII-stripped source document(s) - the actual KB article, form, transcript, or manual the model was supposed to ground in, with personal info redacted to synthetic tokens (you see "Customer A," not a real name). This is what you check the response against. Without it you cannot judge grounding.
  3. The model's response - what the model actually produced.
  4. The rubric - a list of atomic, independent check items, each a should or should not statement. You mark each ✅ Pass, ❌ Fail, or ⚠️ Unsure.

Take your time - a typical review is about an hour: read the source document fully, read the response, then check each rubric item by verifying it against the source, not against your prior knowledge. The source document is ground truth; if the response and the source disagree, the response is wrong.

The rules:

  • Grade each item on its own. Do not let a model's good performance on item 1 influence item 2. Each item is independent.
  • Match the statement exactly as written. If the item says "the response should state the return window is 14 days," you mark ✅ only if the response actually states 14 days - not 21, not "a few weeks," not "per policy." Close is not pass.
  • Verify against the source, not your memory. If the source says "USB-C supports up to 1440p" and the response says "USB-C is fine for 4K," that's a ❌ - even if you personally think some USB-C cables do support 4K. The source is ground truth for this task.
  • Use ⚠️ Unsure sparingly - for genuine ambiguity (the response is contradictory, or the item is unclear). It is not a shortcut when you're tired. Unsure items go to a senior adjudicator; chronic Unsure use lowers your IAA score and your bonus.
  • Write a one-line justification for every ✅ and every ❌, quoting the part of the response (or the source) that supports your call. This deters guess-rating, helps adjudication, and - when the judge disagreed with you - tells us exactly where the judge failed.
  • Do not rate for "vibes." A response that sounds confident and well-formatted but states the wrong return window fails that item. Style is not substance. (This is the single most important rule for calibration - if you pass polished-but-wrong responses, the judge learns to do the same.)

A fully-worked example

Task prompt (RAG):

Customer message: "I bought a monitor last month and I'm not getting the full resolution. Is there a fix, or can I return it?"

Knowledge base (sample 32" 4K monitor user guide): "For 4K resolution, use DisplayPort or HDMI. USB-C supports up to 1440p only. Returns accepted within 14 days of delivery, unopened or in original condition."

Model response:

"Try a different cable - USB-C is usually fine for 4K. You can return it within 30 days if you have the original packaging."

The rubric, and how you grade each item:

#Rubric item (atomic)Your gradeJustification
1The response should recommend DisplayPort or HDMI for 4K.❌ Fail"Recommends USB-C instead; never mentions DisplayPort/HDMI."
2The response should not state USB-C supports 4K.❌ Fail"States 'USB-C is usually fine for 4K' - directly contradicts the KB."

Notice three things about this example. First, the model sounds helpful and confident - and is wrong on three of four items. That is exactly the failure mode we're measuring; your job is to catch it, not be persuaded by tone. Second, a single response yields multiple independent grades - the rubric is the unit, not the response. Third, every grade has a one-line quote.

The authoring job - writing a good task and rubric

When you're on the Authoring track, you will write tasks plus their answer keys. A good task has three parts and one discipline:

  1. A realistic prompt. Write it the way a real user would - messy, with missing info, occasionally ambiguous. Clean synthetic prompts let models cheat.
  2. The ground-truth context the model should use (the KB article, the form, the code repo state). This is locked as gold before grading.
  3. The rubric - decomposed into atomic, independently-checkable items.

The discipline - atomicity. Every rubric item must check one thing. Bad: "The response should correctly cover the return policy." (Three things hidden in one - window length, condition requirement, restocking fee - and graders will disagree.) Good: split it into three items, one per fact. If you can imagine a response that half-passes an item, the item is not atomic; split it.

Write items as observable statements, not internal states. Bad: "The response should understand the customer's situation." Good: "The response should state the 14-day return window." If you can't point to a sentence in the response that satisfies the item, the item is ungradeable - rewrite it.

Author one negative example per task - a plausible-looking response that fails on substance. This teaches raters what a convincing but wrong answer looks like, which is the whole point.

§3.4b The judge-auditing job - when the judge and humans disagree

On the judge-audit track (senior raters), you review cases where the automated judge's grade disagrees with the human panel's. For each, you decide why and route the fix:

  • The rubric item was ambiguous → flag it for rewrite (the rewrite rule, §2.3). This is the most common finding and the highest-value one.
  • The judge was wrong (misread the response, hallucinated a check, applied the wrong source) → log it as a judge failure with the correct grade + justification. These aggregate into the judge's per-criterion reliability score and drive judge re-prompting before the next release.
  • The response was genuinely borderline (reasonable raters could disagree) → record the adjudicated grade and mark the item as "hard" so it's weighted appropriately in calibration.

This is the loop that turns human ratings into a better judge - and it's why the gold sample exists. Without it, the judge drifts silently.

Edge cases - what to do when it's not clean

The response is self-contradictory (says 14 days in one paragraph, 30 in another): grade the item by the most harmful statement the customer would act on, and note the contradiction in your justification. Flag for adjudication with ⚠️ if it's genuinely unresolvable.

The response is missing the information entirely (neither confirms nor denies): that's a ❌ Fail for any "should state…" item. Silence is not a pass.

The rubric item itself seems ambiguous (you and another grader read it differently): grade your honest reading, then flag the item in the feedback field. We track item-level agreement - items that split graders get rewritten, not retrained around (§2.3). Your flags make the benchmark better.

The model leaked or did something unsafe (PII, harmful instruction): mark the dedicated safety item ❌ and use the escalation flag. Do not grade around it.

When in doubt, grade honestly and flag. Never mark ✅ to "be nice" to a model, and never mark ❌ because you're annoyed by the model's tone. Both corrupt the data.

How you're evaluated (and paid)

  • Gold items (~5% of what you rate are secretly pre-answered) measure your accuracy directly.
  • Agreement with other raters (IAA, §2.3) measures consistency; agreement with the judge on clear items measures calibration.
  • Your bonus is tied to both - agree with consensus, hit the gold, earn more. Drift or guess-rating lowers both.
  • Re-qualification every 4-6 weeks keeps everyone calibrated; it's not punitive, it's how we keep the benchmark trustworthy as task types evolve.

The planning scenario uses a $100/hour base rate plus an unpriced quality bonus. Hourly pay is the initial pilot arm for deep review; compare it with capped per-assignment or quota designs before standardizing the compensation model.

§5 QA / Rate-and-Review Process

Qualification (§4) gets a contributor in the door. QA keeps the operation honest while it runs. The classic pitfall here is overly complex workflows and redundant review layers - so the QA design is parallel sampling and dashboards, not a serial review gate on every item. Nothing grades the same item twice for the same purpose (that was settled in §2.2); QA audits the graders and the auto-scorer from the side.

The five QA detectors

QA runs five independent detectors in parallel:

~5% of every rater's queue is planned as secretly pre-answered gold items. Per-rater gold accuracy is one direct signal, but a drop may reflect drift, harder item mix, changed rubrics, or sampling noise. Alert only after the task-type minimum observation count is met and the confidence-aware rule fires.

A stratified sample of every rater's work is re-rated by a QA Reviewer who doesn't know the original grade. The sample rate is set inside the modeled assignment volume, not added as unbudgeted work. Disagreements feed the diagnostic record and, if systematic after controlling for item mix, trigger coaching.

Every rater's agreement-with-peer-panel is tracked per task type and per item, longitudinally. A rater trending down is flagged before a release is corrupted.

Because auto-scorers can be wrong, QA plans to re-grade a stratified 10% sample of auto-scored items by hand each release. Those assignments are nested inside the human-assignment envelope in §7. OpenAI's February 23, 2026 audit is a primary example of test-design and grader-validity concerns in coding evaluation; it does not establish a universal error rate for this proposal. If disagreement exceeds a pre-registered task-type threshold, audit the test suite, data, and sampled raters before assigning cause.

QA measures the LLM judge's agreement with the human gold sample per criterion per release. Systematic disagreement triggers diagnosis of the rubric, judge prompt, task distribution, and human panel before intervention (§2.3, §3.4b). The remaining ~85-90% of trajectories lack independent human review, so this sampled calibration is a release gate rather than a guarantee.

Why parallel, not serial: a serial "every item gets a second reviewer" gate would double cost without doubling accuracy (the gold-sample calibration core already gets a 3-rater panel, while the remaining sampled items are checked by 1 expert rater + judge). QA's value is system-level: catching the rater who's drifting, the auto-scorer that's broken, the judge that's mis-calibrated, the item that's ambiguous. That's a sampling + dashboards job.

Reviewer guidance - the QA Reviewer's job

A QA Reviewer does five things daily:

  • Run the audit sample. Blind-double-rate the stratified sample; record disagreements with a one-line reason.
  • Watch the dashboards. Per-rater gold-accuracy, IAA, and justification-quality trends. Flag any rater crossing a band boundary (§5.3) for the Ops Lead.
  • Run the auto-scorer drift sample. Re-grade the 10% auto-scored sample; if a task type's checker drift exceeds threshold, file a test-suite audit.
  • Run the judge-calibration readout. Aggregate judge-vs-human agreement per criterion; flag criteria below threshold for rewrite or judge re-prompting. This is the highest-leverage QA output - it directly improves the volume scorer.
  • Triage flags. Review ⚠️ Unsure escalations and item-ambiguity flags from raters (§3.5); route ambiguous items to the Rubric Refiner for rewriting (§5.6).

A QA Reviewer reports to the Ops Lead, not to throughput - they can pull a rater, pause a task type, or block a release on judge-miscalibration without asking permission from the release schedule. That independence is what makes QA able to catch problems instead of rationalize them.

Rating criteria - the per-worker quality score and bands

Every evaluator gets a rolling pilot quality score updated daily. The weights and action bands below are provisional policy choices to validate against later gold performance and adjudicated errors:

Quality score = 0.5 × gold-accuracy + 0.3 × IAA (agreement with peer panel) + 0.2 × justification-quality

(Justification-quality = fraction of grades whose one-line justification is specific and correct, sampled by QA.)

The score maps to four bands with explicit actions; internal boundaries are lower-inclusive and upper-exclusive:

🟢 Gold - 0.95 ≤ score ≤ 1.00

Consistently excellent; agrees with consensus, nails gold.

Action: Eligible for Adjudicator track; max bonus.

🟡 Standard - ≥ 0.90 and < 0.95

Reliable; the operating core.

Action: Full bonus; normal queue.

🟠 Watch - ≥ 0.85 and < 0.90

Slipping; some drift or sloppiness.

Action: Coaching this cycle; bonus reduced; queue continues.

🔴 Remove - 0 ≤ score < 0.85 (sustained 2 cycles)

Below the provisional operating band; diagnose before removal.

Action: Pulled from queue; re-qualify or exit.

The bands are longitudinal, not single-batch - a grader doesn't get removed for one bad day. Sustained 2-cycle decline triggers the Remove action, and the cycle threshold gives a slipping grader time to recover with coaching.

The escalation / removal ladder

When a grader crosses into Watch or below:

  1. Coaching (Watch band): a QA Reviewer reviews a sample of the grader's disagreements with the grader, item by item. Often the fix is a single rubric item the grader misread - coaching closes it.
  2. Re-qualification (Watch band, not improving after coaching): pull from queue, re-take the qualification gate (§4) on a fresh draw. Re-enter at Standard if passed.
  3. Exit (Remove band, sustained): remove from the task endorsement for the cycle only after sufficient observations and diagnosis show persistent, material error. Re-apply next cycle.

This ladder is driven by the quality score, not by a reviewer's gut. That's the difference between a defensible QA process and a vibes-based one.

Collusion and bad-actor detection

A 1,000-person remote workforce will attract bad actors. QA runs dedicated detectors:

  • Answer-pattern clustering (JMLR framework): flag graders whose answer sequences are suspiciously identical to another grader's (copy-paste grading, multi-accounting).
  • Reputation-history review (Gadiraju et al., CHI 2015 author copy, on malicious behavior in crowdsourced surveys): prior acceptance rates alone may not separate trustworthy from untrustworthy workers, so use them only as a weak investigation feature alongside observed task behavior.
  • Device / IP fingerprinting: cap accounts per device and per payment instrument; flag impossible geography changes.
  • The justification requirement (§3.2) is itself an anti-collusion measure - copy-paste graders can't fabricate specific one-line quotes at scale.

A confirmed collusion flag is an immediate Remove, no coaching cycle - it's fraud, not drift.

The item-level quality loop

QA doesn't just rate graders - it diagnoses items. Low item-level α can come from rubric ambiguity, rater error, class prevalence, or genuine task difficulty (§2.3). So:

  • QA flags low or imprecisely estimated item-level α for diagnostic review; 0.667 is an initial attention guideline, not an automatic rewrite law.
  • The reviewer checks prevalence, rater-specific patterns, item difficulty, and rubric wording. Rewrite only when ambiguity or construction error is supported.
  • Revised items re-enter the next release's pool after fresh validation; historical grades on the old item are quarantined from the leaderboard.

When ambiguity is the cause, fixing one item can prevent repeated downstream disagreement. The pilot should measure whether item revision, rater coaching, or additional sampling delivers the best error reduction per hour.

§7.7 Cost levers (how to scale down)

If budget is tighter than the ~$8.05M/6mo planning scenario, the levers - in order of least quality damage - are:

  1. Drop task types (run 3 instead of 6): ~40-50% variable-bench reduction; narrows the routing readout but each remaining type stays statistically sound.
  2. Reduce the gold-sample fraction (15% → 8%): linear savings on rating; trades calibration power for cost. Keep the 3-rater calibration core intact.
  3. Reduce model count per release (benchmark 5 instead of 10): linear savings on authoring and judge compute; the routing readout covers fewer options.
  4. Tighten the 6-month release count (5 → 3 releases): amortizes the fixed core over fewer releases but cuts variable bench proportionally.

Integrity controls The proposed design keeps the 3-rater calibration core, judge-auditing loop, auto-scorer drift sampling, and PII-redaction QA in scope. Cutting them would reduce the evidence available to detect judge or data failures; whether the resulting signal is fit for routing must be demonstrated by release-specific audit results.

§8 Essays

Two short essays on coding-agent selection.

8.1 Choosing daily-driver coding agents

I don't have one daily driver - I have a task-conditional rotation, and I think that's the honest answer for this proposal. The framing of this document is that the right model for the right task may outperform one model for everything; the same hypothesis applies at the agent layer.

My actual selection logic, roughly in order:

  1. What's the dominant cost of this task - exploration or generation? For navigating an unfamiliar codebase, triaging a bug, or understanding where a change needs to go, I want an agent with strong retrieval and long-context and weak "eagerness to edit." For a well-scoped mechanical change, I want the opposite - fast, decisive editing. Using an eager editor for exploration produces a lot of confident, premature diffs.
  2. How verifiable is the result? If the task has a clean test or compile signal (type-checked refactor, migration script with a target schema), I'll let a more autonomous agent run and trust the signal. If verification is judgment-based ("is this API design good?"), I want an agent that drafts and explains and hands back to me early - autonomy without verification is liability.
  3. Tool reliability in this repo. An agent is only as good as its tools in your environment. I'll run a small calibration task first (a known issue I've already solved by hand) and watch: does it call tools correctly, recover from errors, or get stuck in loops? The §0 failures are exactly this - models that are smart in the abstract but can't paginate a search or read a shipment record in practice. Tool competence is local, not general.
  4. Cost discipline. For high-volume boilerplate, a cheaper/smaller agent often wins; for the one gnarly bug, the frontier model is worth it. This is the routing thesis applied to my own workflow.

The meta-point: I choose by running small experiments and reading the trajectories, not by leaderboard rank. A model that tops SWE-bench but burns reasoning tokens hallucinating context in my repo is the wrong pick. Taste here is the willingness to calibrate locally rather than defer to a public score.

8.2 The gaps between today's coding agents and fully autonomous SWE

Four gaps, most directly visible in the public benchmarks (§0 especially):

  1. The grounding/spec gap. Some failures arise from finding and using the right information, not only from reasoning capacity. The illustrative patterns include inferring facts from partial cues, missing product-specific manual details, or failing to paginate. Better retrieval tooling and routing to grounding specialists are hypotheses to test alongside stronger general models.
  2. The self-verification gap. Agents can fail to recognize when their output is wrong. Published audits have identified test-design and grader-validity issues in coding evaluations, including OpenAI's February 2026 SWE-bench Verified audit; those findings do not define a universal error rate. A separate verifier is therefore an arm to test where the cost and error consequences justify it, not a presumed universal architecture.
  3. The judgment-about-when-not-to-act gap. The illustrative "execute a conditional branch that does not apply" pattern represents one operational risk. Real software work includes dead ends, unnecessary features, and scope changes; production trials should measure whether an agent can stop, request clarification, and preserve state before granting broader autonomy.
  4. Long-horizon state and memory. Long tool-use sequences can expose state loss, repeated work, and contradictions. Two measurements must stay separate: pass^k measures consistency across repeated end-to-end trials, while step-level or phase-level survival analysis measures where failures compound within one trajectory. Sequential failure must be estimated from observed trajectories; it cannot be inferred from pass^k alone.

The unifying thread: these are not "the model isn't smart enough" problems. They're grounding, verification, judgment, and state problems - exactly the dimensions where a specialist + composition + better tooling beats a bigger frontier model. Which is why this benchmark is built to measure exactly those dimensions.
